One tap. Total exclusion. No questions asked.

A proof of concept for a block button that asks first, and never slows down the people who need it most.

The problem

Blocking is one of the most powerful actions a social network offers: it silently cuts a person off, completely and instantly. Platforms hand it out with a single tap, no context, and no follow-up.

The same button protects someone from a stalker and lets someone avoid an awkward conversation. The platform can't tell the two apart, so it learns nothing about either.

The principle

Friction should scale inversely with danger.

Low stakes: slow down, offer gentler tools. High stakes: block immediately, ask questions afterwards. The block button opens a short questionnaire whose questions get more specific as urgency rises, and it ends with a report to the platform when the situation calls for one.

  • Minor discomfort: suggest mute, restrict or a 30-day snooze. Block stays available.
  • Real conflict: block, with report offered.
  • Harassment or repeated contact: block and report recommended, pre-filled from the answers.
  • Safety at risk: instant block, priority report, evidence preserved, support resources. No questions first.

Whose job is this?

A social network's job is to manage social conflict. If every dispute is handed to a one-tap exclusion button, the platform has outsourced its core function to its users. Social isn't an optional feature of a social network. A platform that doesn't want to handle it properly should stop calling itself one and say what it is: an advertising business with a friends list.

What the research suggests

  • Blocking is experienced very differently on each side. A 2026 conference poster (N = 130) reports relief and restored control for people who block, and confusion, rejection and lower need satisfaction for people who are blocked. APS 2026 poster
  • Online ostracism threatens basic needs (belonging, esteem, control, meaning) across age groups, which is why a silent block isn't a neutral act. Summary of Abrams and Williams's findings
  • Design "friction" that makes people stop and think has been welcomed by civil-rights groups as a way to reduce harassment without relying on punishment alone. ADL statement on Instagram's 2019 changes

What this isn't

This is a flow prototype, not a validated clinical tool. The wording, the scoring and the thresholds are placeholders to be tested with psychologists, harassment-survivor advocates and trust & safety teams. Its main design constraint: friction must never stand between a person in danger and the block. That is why the "I feel unsafe" answer, on the very first screen, blocks instantly.

Open questions

These are deliberately left open. This is a proposal to discuss, not a finished answer.

  • Should the blocked person be told? A neutral notice might reduce confusion and the sense of being silently erased. It might also escalate a dangerous situation, or tip off someone who shouldn't know. The prototype takes no position.
  • Who sees the answers? The questionnaire creates sensitive data. Retention, access and use limits would have to be designed with privacy experts.
  • How should thresholds be set, and by whom? The scoring here is a placeholder. A real one needs evidence, testing across cultures, and an audit trail.
  • Can it be gamed? People could pick whatever answer unlocks the outcome they want. What would make the flow robust without making it punitive?
